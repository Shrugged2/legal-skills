# Where the Argument Breaks

Read the draft as a judge who must give reasons for the requested order. Locate the first consequential step the judge could reasonably decline to take. Show the writer how to repair it. A well-supported passage can pass in a single sentence.

## Run a review

1. Identify the requested ruling, the question this passage undertakes to answer, the procedural posture, and the materials supplied. Infer these from the draft where possible. Ask for missing context only if it would change the diagnosis and useful conditional feedback is impossible.
2. Reconstruct the writer's best argument in ordinary language. Work backward from the proposed ruling to the governing requirement, the decisive fact, and the inference connecting them. Track independent grounds separately. A factor test, an alternative ground, and a conjunctive rule require different treatment.
3. Apply the checklist below. Look in the rest of the supplied draft for an answer before flagging an omission. Accept fair inferences, ordinary background premises, supported concessions, and clear cross-references. Credit a reasonable inference when the writer need only establish that a factfinder could draw it.
4. Test the strongest response available on these materials. If the opponent supplied a brief, distinguish its actual position from an objection you devised. Ask whether a judge could accept the writer's point and still rule against the writer. Identify the remaining ground only if it matters to the relief sought.
5. Report the smallest effective repair. Rewrite a sentence when the supplied materials support it. When repair requires evidence or authority, identify precisely what the writer needs to find and what narrower claim survives without it. Preserve the writer's voice and concessions.

**Mid-draft mode.** For a paragraph or section, lead with its most consequential weakness. Quote the turning words, explain the missing step, and give one repair. Identify what works when it helps the writer preserve it. Treat omitted sections as outside the review.

**Whole-brief mode.** For a full draft, start with the issue most likely to affect the disposition. Rank distinct findings by consequence, cite a heading or page for each, and group repeated instances. Differentiate a defect that defeats the requested order, one that weakens a particular route, and one the rest of the brief already cures. End with the edit to make first. Report additional material defects even if they fall outside the checklist.

**Evidence discipline.** Distinguish a flaw visible in the reasoning from a proposition that requires source verification. If the cited exhibit or opinion is unavailable, specify the passage to check. Never invent its contents, a pincite, an unraised defense, or a jurisdiction-specific rule. Attribute supplied assertions to their source. Reserve claims of legal or factual verification for materials actually checked.

## Bad-brief checklist

Use this section on paper or during a review. For each issue, mark the weakest link and write the proposed fix beside it.

| Check | Question for the writer | Smallest useful repair |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Requested decision | What exactly would the court decide if this section succeeds? | Replace an abstract thesis with the ruling and the reason the court can reach it. |
| 2. Governing rule | Did you turn a factor into a requirement, omit an exception, or glide past a contested element? | Restore the operative language. Apply the disputed part to a specific fact. |
| 3. Use of authority | Did the court decide your proposition, assume it, report a party's contention, or leave it open? | Identify what the court resolved. Narrow the verb or supply authority that decides the point. |
| 4. Case comparison | Which similarity or difference explains the prior result? | Connect one fact in each case to the same legal reason. Address a material distinction. |
| 5. Record support | Does the cited evidence establish the actor, timing, event, and degree of certainty your sentence claims? | Quote or pinpoint the part carrying the disputed detail. Attribute testimony and qualify disputed facts where posture requires. |
| 6. Missing premise | Could a reader accept your evidence and reasonably reject your inference? Does that matter under the applicable burden? | State the missing premise, supply its basis, or narrow the conclusion to the inference the evidence permits. |
| 7. Serious objection | Could the opponent concede the point you attack and still prevail on its actual theory? | Give the best remaining objection one fair sentence. Answer it where the contested premise first matters. |
| 8. Burden and review | Who decides the disputed point, who bears the burden, and how much must that party show at this stage? | Recast the application around the decision the court may make on this record. |
| 9. Independent ground | If the court accepts your best argument, does another sufficient basis for the same outcome survive? | Address that ground, show why it depends on the first, or narrow the relief sought. |
| 10. Remedy | What connects the demonstrated error to this particular order? | Identify the necessary consequence under the supplied standard and the facts supporting it. Ask for the precise disposition. |

## Calibration examples

All facts, rules, and authorities below are fictional. Each pair changes one material feature. To reproduce the exercise, give a reviewer the same instruction, “Review this passage using the supplied material,” in a fresh session for each variant. Withhold the expected findings. Score whether the reviewer identifies the disputed proposition, proposes a supported repair, and allows the sound version to stand.

### 1. What did the manufacturer learn?

Draft for both runs: “Before the accident, the manufacturer had received a report that this latch model opened under load.”

- A. Record: A service email received before the accident says the paint chipped on this model.
- B. Record: The same email says the latch on this model opened while supporting a load.

Expected: In A, timing and receipt are supported. The subject of the report is wrong. Describe the paint complaint or find a report about opening under load. In B, the sentence accurately describes the report. Demanding proof that the defect actually existed would test a claim the writer never made.

### 2. What made the precedent come out that way?

Supplied opinion: In Vale, the courier owed a duty to safeguard a parcel because the sender surrendered the only key to the courier's locked compartment. Draft: “As in Vale, the sender placed the parcel in a locked compartment. The courier therefore undertook the same duty.”

- A. Stipulation: Only the sender kept a key.
- B. Stipulation: The sender handed the only key to the courier. The previous sentence in the draft expressly states that fact.

Expected: In A, the shared lock masks a decisive difference in control. Distinguish Vale and identify what other basis could establish a duty. In B, read the sentences together. The preceding sentence supplies the material relationship. Leave the argument intact.

### 3. Would winning the point change the order?

Supplied rule: Each independently sufficient ground must be displaced before the panel may vacate. Draft for both runs: “The order rests on late service. The stipulated return shows timely service. The panel should vacate.”

- A. Order: Dismissed for late service and, independently, failure to pay the fee. The fee ruling is unchallenged.
- B. Order: Dismissed solely for late service.

Expected: In A, the draft misstates the order. Proving timely service leaves the fee ruling intact. Correct the description and address the fee ruling if a supported challenge is available. In B, the stipulated return answers the sole ground. Accept the requested disposition under the supplied rule.

## Sources and design choices

- John Delaney, *Learning Legal Reasoning*, “Briefing and the Case Method,” printed p. 3. Delaney separates the components of an opinion. Check 3 puts that distinction to work by asking which component a brief has cited as the court's decision.
- Linda L. Berger and Kathryn M. Stanchi, *Legal Persuasion*, ch. 11, pp. 88–89. Their account distinguishes resemblance in individual features from resemblance in relationships and purpose. Check 4 uses that distinction to test which fact carries the comparison.
- Bryan A. Garner, *The Winning Brief*, tip 88, explanation. Garner connects support for the advocate's premises with a deliberate answer to serious counterarguments. Checks 6 and 7 turn that sequence into questions a skeptical reader can ask.

The checklist, repair suggestions, paired examples, and ranking procedure are original teaching materials. The three passages above supply writing principles. Apply governing law from the matter under review.
