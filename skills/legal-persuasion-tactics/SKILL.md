# Legal Persuasion Tactics

Recommend the strongest move for this decision, give the writer words to try, and explain what the move costs. The repertoire below is self-contained. If the user supplies additional readings, use relevant passages and identify any new tactic's source separately.

**Scope.** This skill addresses judges, appellate panels, agencies, and negotiating counterparties. Jury persuasion follows different rules (no argument in opening, and theme-building by repetition) and is outside it. Say so if asked.

**Candor floor.** Disclosure of directly adverse controlling authority is required by the rules of professional conduct, not a tactical choice. The tactical choice is where to place it and how to answer it. No tactic here permits overstating a fact, a holding, or the record.

## Choose the move

1. Identify who must do what, in which forum, at what stage, and why that person might reasonably hesitate. Infer from the document before asking. If no problem is supplied, ask for the passage or facts, the audience, and the desired result. If a material detail is unknown, proceed conditionally when possible.
2. Identify the strongest available fact, authority, concession, or institutional reason. Quote or locate it. Separate materials actually reviewed from the user's description. Identify the strongest adverse fact. Find the obstacle: disagreement about the rule, disbelief about events, a credible rival inference, fear of precedent, cost of the remedy, distrust, or difficulty following the argument. If the obstacle is unclear, run where-the-argument-breaks first.
3. Consider several genuinely different tactics. Compare what each asks the audience to accept, what support it requires, and the best reply it invites. Select one lead move and at most two complementary moves by default. Choose fewer when a direct answer settles the point. For brainstorming, widen the range and group options by tradeoff. A tactic outside the repertoire is fine if it fits better; label it as an additional suggestion.
4. Deliver the recommendation in the user's medium. For a brief, supply a heading or a few usable sentences and where they go. For oral argument, answer the question in the first sentence, then give a reason and the likely follow-up. For negotiation, offer a concrete term or exchange the recipient can accept. For a judicial audience, start from the court's role, the record, and the authority. Do not guess at an individual judge's psychology.
5. For each selected move, state **why here, how to use it, and where it could fail**. Give the opponent's best answer and say whether it changes the recommendation. If useful, name one tempting tactic to drop and why. Keep good language already in the draft. Do not invent quotations, authorities, procedural entitlements, statistics, motives, or evidence. Label stipulations, hypotheticals, and facts needing confirmation. A missing element may call for evidence or narrower relief before any rhetorical work.

## Tactic repertoire

Each row gives a trigger, an original fictional application, and a limit. Adapt the mechanism, not the names or facts. Examples assume only the conditions stated in their row.

### Framing the question and the decision

| Tactic and trigger | Example move | Limit or likely reply |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Foreground the deciding condition. The dispute opens on distracting misconduct. | "The fee turns on when the buyer received notice. The seller's first delivery receipt is dated June 4." Lead with the trigger before recounting the quarrel. | A receipt date alone may not establish first actual notice. Check other channels. |
| 2. Build the theme from a provable contrast. The record is sprawling. | "The labels changed three times. The exclusion stayed the same." Organize each revision around that continuity. | Compare all versions before declaring the language unchanged. |
| 3. Choose the level of generality. The opponent states an attractive but sweeping principle. | Accept the need for inspections, then ask who may authorize *this inspection after consent expires*. | The narrower framing must still account for any independent authorization. |
| 4. Lead with shared ground. The parties disagree about the rule's application. | "Both sides agree that a timely cure preserves the agreement. Their dispute concerns what counted as a cure." | Confirm genuine agreement. Attribute only what the other side actually accepts. |
| 5. Give the court a narrow route. A broad theory would force a difficult institutional choice. | "The panel can enforce the express renewal date and leave implied renewals for a contract that lacks one." | Show that the text and posture permit that disposition. |
| 6. Make the question answerable. A question presented reads like a topic label. | Replace "Whether notice was proper" with "Did the seller give the required ten days when it notified the buyer on Monday and terminated on Friday?" | Include qualifications or exceptions that could change the answer. |
| 7. Argue inside the standard of review. The draft argues the merits as if the court decides them fresh. | As appellee under a deferential standard: "The question is not whether this Court would have weighed the testimony differently. It is whether any evidence supports the finding. Three witnesses do." | Confirm the standard for each issue. Mixed questions may be reviewed differently in parts. |

### Precedent, rules, and institutional consequences

| Tactic and trigger | Example move | Limit or likely reply |
|---|---|---|
| 8. Distinguish on the fact that did the work. A bad case looks almost identical. | "Both parcels were locked. In the earlier case the courier alone held the key. Here the customer kept it." Tie control to the earlier court's reason. | If the opinion turned on possession alone, the key distinction buys little. |
| 9. Analogize by function. Objects differ but play the same legally relevant role. | "The one-time code did the work of the key: whoever controlled it controlled release." | Address shared access, expiration, or overrides if they undermine equivalence. |
| 10. Distinguish what the prior court could decide. The same allegation survived an earlier motion. | "That court assumed receipt on the pleadings. At trial the sender still had to prove it." | Inspect the opinion for an alternative merits holding. |
| 11. Test the opponent's rule on its nearest hard case. A categorical rule sounds harmless. | "If uploading alone counts as notice, does an upload to an account the sender knows is closed start the clock?" | Use a consequence entailed by the actual rule. Give the opponent its best distinction. |
| 12. Offer a stopping point. The court asks "Where does this end?" | "Draw the line at exclusive control. A vendor who retained an override falls outside the proposed rule." | Explain why the line follows the text or rationale and how it can be proved. |
| 13. Compare administration under both rules. Either interpretation is plausible. | "The dated acknowledgment gives both sides one event to prove. An awareness test requires reconstructing who read which message." | Acknowledge the cost of the preferred rule, such as deliberate refusal to acknowledge. |
| 14. Separate the judge's role from the policy choice. The policy dispute is real but unnecessary. | "The safety proposal deserves a hearing. The question today is whether the agency used the hearing procedure the legislature selected." | Show that the procedural point matters under governing law and supports the requested relief. |

### Facts, narrative, and reader attention

| Tactic and trigger | Example move | Limit or likely reply |
|---|---|---|
| 15. Begin at the decision point. Background buries the choice. | Open when the dispatcher received the warning. Then supply the earlier events needed to understand the choices still available. | Preserve the timing of knowledge. Later information cannot supply an earlier motive. |
| 16. Reconstruct a bounded point of view. Hindsight makes a decision seem inevitable. | "From the loading bay, Ruiz could see the signal. The parked trailer blocked his view of the crossing." | Ground sightlines in testimony or exhibits. Visibility alone does not prove perception. |
| 17. Use scene for the hinge and summary for elapsed time. Every event gets equal space. | Compress six routine inspections into a sentence. Slow down for the seventh: who opened the cabinet, what the gauge showed, who signed. | Retain an earlier anomaly if it undercuts the description of routine. |
| 18. Let a concrete detail carry the human consequence. The draft relies on emotional labels. | Replace "the shutdown was devastating" with "The clinic canceled all six dialysis appointments scheduled that morning." | Use only supported details relevant to the decision. A vivid loss does not prove liability. |
| 19. Give competing accounts the same timeline. The parties agree on events but contest sequence. | Place approval, shipment, and rejection on one dated line for each account. Highlight the one disputed timestamp. | Distinguish event time from entry time and mark an unknown explicitly. |
| 20. Use the witness's exact words at the pivot. A paraphrase conceals ambiguity. | If the transcript reads "I saw the bag afterward," quote "afterward" before explaining the limits of the identification. | Include context that changes the meaning. Do not insinuate that an omission is a denial. |

### Opposition, credibility, and rebuttal

| Tactic and trigger | Example move | Limit or likely reply |
|---|---|---|
| 21. Disclose and answer the damaging fact. The other side will feature it. | "Lee missed Tuesday's inspection. The valve had already been removed Monday." Place the concession where the chronology answers it. | If earlier inspections could have mattered, acknowledge that separate theory. |
| 22. Grant the premise and contest its reach. Fighting a sound point costs credibility. | "The officer could see the screen. That establishes opportunity. The log does not show whether she opened this message." | An acknowledgment or independent testimony may bridge the gap. |
| 23. Borrow the opponent's own criterion. Its stated test favors your evidence. | "The carrier asks the court to use the first machine scan. Its own scan places the package at the depot at 8:12." | Read the criterion in context and answer any exception it asserted. |
| 24. Attack the inference while crediting the source. A personal attack would obscure the issue. | "The clerk accurately described the usual practice. She also testified that she did not handle this envelope." | Usual practice may itself support an inference. Address its strength under the applicable standard. |
| 25. Answer the opponent at full strength. A broad rebuttal leaves a narrower theory intact. | "Even if the reminder described the defect perfectly, the contract required it to reach the designated office. Which office received it?" | Verify the designation, any waiver, and actual course of dealing. |
| 26. State what would change your answer. The audience suspects a result-driven rule. | "If the override had remained active, I would agree the customer lacked exclusive control. The stipulated deactivation is why this case comes out differently." | Choose a genuine, legally material concession you can live with. |

### Arrangement, delivery, and practical relief

| Tactic and trigger | Example move | Limit or likely reply |
|---|---|---|
| 27. Order and cut. The draft presents several grounds of uneven strength. | Lead with the ground most likely to win and easiest to grant. Cut a ground whose weakness invites the court to doubt the others. | Preservation may require raising a ground anyway. Keep it short instead of dropping it. |
| 28. Write a heading the decision-maker can reuse. The contents page lists subjects. | Replace "The Audit Log" with "The audit log places approval two hours after shipment." | A creation timestamp may not mark approval. Check what the field means. |
| 29. Unpack an abstraction into actors and actions. A technically accurate sentence hides responsibility. | Replace "authorization was effectuated subsequent to release" with "The supervisor approved the release after the truck left." | Preserve technical terms that affect the operative legal test. |
| 30. Answer first and qualify second. A judge poses an uncomfortable hypothetical. | "Yes, on those facts the buyer loses. Our buyer wins because the seller disabled the only permitted submission channel." | Answer the hypothetical on its own terms before distinguishing the record. |
| 31. Translate the argument into an executable order. The decision-maker accepts the problem but fears a sprawling remedy. | "Require production of the two identified logs for May 1 to 7. Leave the remaining repositories for a showing that those logs are incomplete." | Check remedial authority and whether the limited relief meets the client's objective. |
| 32. Offer a reversible first step. A counterparty resists an irreversible commitment. | "Release the undisputed invoices Friday. Hold the disputed freight charge until both sides compare the signed tickets Tuesday." | Check authority, deadlines, waiver, and whether the proposal shifts risk unfairly. |

## Worked selection: one bad fact, three settings

Fictional supply dispute. The vendor missed the first inspection. Both sides signed a seven-day cure clause. The customer terminated the next morning. Whether the vendor could have cured is disputed.

**For a motion about the contract's timing,** lead with the deciding condition (1) and concede the missed inspection (21). Draft: "The vendor missed the inspection. The agreement gave it seven days to cure. The customer terminated the next morning." Then quote the operative clause, identify when the period began, and address exceptions. A claim that the vendor certainly would have cured invites a factual dispute the timing argument may not require. If incurability is an exception, address it directly.

**For the judge's question, "Must a customer wait through an unsafe condition?"** start with the safety premise and identify the boundary (12, 30). If the agreement separately authorizes suspension: "No. The customer could suspend deliveries immediately. The seven days governed permanent termination." Without that provision or another established basis, do not assume suspension authority. Explain which question the text resolves and which remains open.

**For a negotiation to resume deliveries,** lead with a reversible proposal (32): "We will pay for an independent inspection tomorrow. If the unit passes, release the next shipment. If it fails, keep the hold in place while we address the defect." Use this only if the client authorizes the offer. The same record now calls for a way to manage risk, not a speech about who breached first.
