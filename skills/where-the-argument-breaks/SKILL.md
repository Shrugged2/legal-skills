# Where the Argument Breaks

Read the draft as a judge who must give reasons for the requested order. Find the step the judge could reasonably decline to take that matters most to the ruling. Show the writer how to repair it. A well-supported passage can pass in a single sentence.

## Run a review

1. Identify the requested ruling, the question the passage undertakes to answer, the procedural posture, and the materials supplied. Infer these from the draft where possible. Ask for missing context only if it would change the diagnosis and useful conditional feedback is impossible.
2. Reconstruct the writer's best argument in ordinary language. Work backward from the proposed ruling to the governing requirement, the decisive fact, and the inference connecting them. Track independent grounds separately. A factor test, an alternative ground, and a conjunctive rule need different treatment.
3. Apply the checklist below. Look in the rest of the draft for an answer before flagging an omission. Accept fair inferences, ordinary background premises, supported concessions, and clear cross-references. Credit a reasonable inference when the writer need only show that a factfinder could draw it.
4. Test the strongest response available on these materials. If the opponent's brief is supplied, distinguish its actual position from an objection you devised. Ask whether a judge could accept the writer's point and still rule against the writer.
5. Report the smallest effective repair. Rewrite a sentence when the supplied materials support it. When the repair needs evidence or authority, say precisely what the writer must find and what narrower claim survives without it. Keep the writer's voice and concessions.

**Which flaw leads.** Rank by consequence to the ruling, not by position in the chain. Lead with the defect most likely to change the disposition. Then list earlier weak links in chain order so the writer can see how the argument reads from the top.

**When a small repair won't do.** If the defect runs through the whole section (the wrong rule, the wrong level of generality, or a conclusion no version of the facts supports), say that sentence-level repairs will not fix it and outline the rebuild instead.

**Mid-draft mode.** For a paragraph or section, lead with its most consequential weakness. Quote the turning words, explain the missing step, and give one repair. Say what works when that helps the writer keep it. Treat omitted sections as outside the review.

**Whole-brief mode.** Start with the issue most likely to affect the disposition. Rank distinct findings by consequence, cite a heading or page for each, and group repeated instances. Distinguish a defect that defeats the requested order, one that weakens a particular route, and one the rest of the brief already cures. End with the edit to make first. Report material defects outside the checklist too.

**Evidence discipline.** Separate a flaw visible in the reasoning from a proposition that needs source verification. If a cited exhibit or opinion is unavailable, mark the proposition Unverified and specify the passage to check. Never invent its contents, a pincite, an unraised defense, or a jurisdiction-specific rule. Claim verification only for materials actually checked.

## Checklist

| Check | Question for the writer | Smallest useful repair |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Requested decision | What exactly would the court decide if this section succeeds? | Replace an abstract thesis with the ruling and the reason the court can reach it. |
| 2. Governing rule | Did you turn a factor into a requirement, omit an exception, or glide past a contested element? | Restore the operative language. Apply the disputed part to a specific fact. |
| 3. Use of authority | Did the court decide your proposition, assume it, report a party's contention, or leave it open? | Identify what the court resolved. Narrow the verb or supply authority that decides the point. |
| 4. Case comparison | Which similarity or difference explains the prior result? | Connect one fact in each case to the same legal reason. Address a material distinction. |
| 5. Record support | Does the cited evidence establish the actor, timing, event, and certainty your sentence claims? | Pinpoint the part carrying the disputed detail. Attribute testimony and qualify disputed facts where the posture requires. |
| 6. Missing premise | Could a reader accept your evidence and reasonably reject your inference? Does that matter under the burden? | State the missing premise, supply its basis, or narrow the conclusion to what the evidence permits. |
| 7. Serious objection | Could the opponent concede the point you attack and still prevail on its actual theory? | Give the best remaining objection one fair sentence. Answer it where the contested premise first matters. |
| 8. Burden, review, preservation | Who decides the point, who bears the burden, how much must be shown at this stage, and was the argument raised and ruled on below? | Recast the application around the decision the court may make on this record. On appeal, cite where the argument was preserved, or address the consequence if it was not. |
| 9. Independent ground | If the court accepts your best argument, does another sufficient basis for the same outcome survive? | Address that ground, show why it depends on the first, or narrow the relief sought. |
| 10. Harm and remedy | Even if the error occurred, did it affect the outcome, and what connects it to this particular order? | Show the prejudice under the governing harm standard and the facts supporting it. Ask for the precise disposition. |

## Calibration principles

- A sentence that accurately describes its source passes, even if the underlying fact is disputed. Do not test a claim the writer never made.
- Read adjacent sentences together. If the preceding sentence supplies the material fact, the comparison stands.
- Winning the attacked point is not enough if an unchallenged independent ground supports the order. Check the order's actual grounds.
- When the record answers the only ground, accept the requested disposition. Do not manufacture a second objection.
