# Case Briefing

Produce something a lawyer can cite, quote, distinguish, or extend. A practitioner's brief answers four questions:

- **Can I cite this?** Court, date, binding weight, holding versus dicta, later treatment.
- **What does it hold, exactly?** The narrow rule on these facts and the broader rule in the reasoning.
- **What reasoning can I use?** The court's logic, quotable passages, and policy justifications.
- **How does it connect?** To the line of cases, to the user's facts, and to the arguments available.

## Status of every claim about the opinion

Use the suite's three labels for any claim that depends on a source:

- **Verified:** traced to a passage you read in the supplied opinion. Give the page or paragraph.
- **Contradicted:** the opinion says otherwise, or the claimed passage is absent.
- **Unverified:** you have not seen the source that would settle it (later treatment, a cited case, a missing page). Say what to check.

An Unverified item never becomes Verified because the answer seems familiar. If you are working from an excerpt or a summary, say so at the top.

## Read before briefing

Read with these questions in hand. Read a long opinion twice: once for its shape, once for detail.

1. What question is the court actually answering? Courts do not always state it cleanly.
2. What did the court decide? The disposition, not the aspirational language.
3. Why does the answer make sense to the court, in the court's own terms?
4. Which facts did the court rely on? Language like "here, the plaintiff had..." usually signals an outcome-determinative fact. Facts mentioned but not used in the reasoning are background.
5. What did the court decline to decide? Express reservations ("we do not reach...") often matter more than they look.

## Single-case brief

Use this structure unless the request calls for less. Collapse any section with nothing to say, or note its absence when the absence is informative.

1. **Citation and authority.** Court, date, panel if known. Binding weight in the user's likely forum: court of last resort, intermediate appellate court, or trial court; binding, persuasive, or foreign. Later treatment only if you have checked a citator or a supplied source. Otherwise mark it Unverified: "Not checked for later treatment; confirm before citing."
2. **Procedural posture and standard of review.** What ruling was under review. A holding on a motion to dismiss tells you which allegations survive, not which facts win at trial. A holding under de novo review carries different force than one under abuse-of-discretion review.
3. **Facts in two layers.** Outcome-determinative facts, drawn from what the court emphasized in its reasoning. Then background, kept minimal.
4. **Issues.** State each as a question joining the rule and the facts. Separate multiple issues and mark which were dispositive and which were alternative holdings.
5. **Holding, narrow and broad.** The narrow holding is tied to these facts: "On facts X, Y, and Z, the court held..." The broad holding is the principle as the court stated it. Later courts choose between them, so the user needs both.
6. **Reasoning.** The chain that produced the holding: the governing test, how the court applied it, the policy it relied on, the precedent it leaned on, and the authority it distinguished or rejected.
7. **Judgment.** The disposition in practical terms ("reversed summary judgment; remanded for trial on the negligence claim").
8. **Dicta worth noting.** Views on adjacent issues, hypotheticals, comments on other lines of authority. Label each as dicta.
9. **Usable quotes.** Two to five short quotations that state the rule or deliver the policy, with pincites. Quote only from text you have read.
10. **Practical notes.** Fact patterns the case would control; the narrowest reading an opponent could advance; facts that would take a case outside the holding; extension arguments; weaknesses such as thin reasoning or later criticism. This section is what separates a practitioner brief from a student brief.

## Short requests

- **"What's the holding in X":** narrow and broad holding, one sentence each.
- **"What does X stand for":** broad holding, one key quote, and a sentence on how it is usually deployed.
- **"Can I cite X for Y":** the narrow holding; whether Y falls inside it or requires extension; the extension argument; the risk.
- **"Best quote for X":** the quotes, with pincites and the context that could change their meaning.

## Synthesis of several cases

Do not stack individual briefs. Produce one analysis:

1. **The question** the cases collectively address.
2. **The rule,** stated only as precisely as the cases support. If they do not converge, say so. A fractured rule is a finding.
3. **Elements or factors,** each with the cases that established or clarified it.
4. **Trajectory.** The foundational case; the cases that extended, limited, or clarified it; the outliers and why.
5. **Facts across cases.** "Where the fact was X, the outcome was A. Where it was Y, the outcome was B. The apparent dividing line is Z." This is often more useful than the rule statement.
6. **Tensions and open questions,** including splits and issues no case has squarely decided.
7. **Summary table.** One row per case: cite, court, year, posture, key fact, holding, notable dicta.
8. **Practical notes.** Which cases best support which arguments, which carry the most weight in the user's forum, and which adverse cases need distinguishing.

Each case is one link in a chain. Ask what this case adds given the cases around it. Any rule can be read narrowly (tied to its facts) or broadly (as articulated). Track both readings and note where the cases themselves disagree about which applies.

## Check before returning

- Does the narrow holding track the actual facts, or has it drifted into abstraction?
- Does the broad holding appear in the reasoning, or did you infer it?
- Is every claim about the opinion tied to a passage?
- Are dicta labeled? Does the holding's reach match the posture?
- Are the quotes usable, and are they verbatim?
- In synthesis, do the cases actually converge on the rule you stated?

## Limits

This skill does not replace reading the opinion, does not act as a citator, and does not find authority. It briefs what it is given.
